Mold Removal Businesses in Holliston, MA: How to Choose and What to Ask

Massachusetts does not license mold remediation, so you have to vet the company yourself. Here is a practical checklist, red flags and a model written scope.

By the Holliston Water Damage Pros team | Published October 29, 2026

☎ (508) 446-9244

The short answer: vet the paperwork, not the pitch

To choose among mold removal businesses in Holliston, MA, ask for a Statement of Qualifications, references, proof of insurance and a written scope that names containment, removal, drying and verification. Compare those documents before you compare price or phone manners.

The reason is that anyone can call themselves a mold specialist here. The state Department of Public Health's homeowner guide says there are no licenses required to conduct mold remediation in Massachusetts.

What the lack of a state license means for you

In states with licensing, a license number and an exam stand between you and an unqualified contractor. Here, nothing does. Your protection is the quality of the paper trail you ask for.

Ask for a Statement of Qualifications that describes training, experience and the standards the company follows. Ask for references from similar jobs and call them. Confirm general liability insurance and workers' compensation coverage in writing. Licensed and insured is the baseline, not a bonus.

Questions to ask every company

  • Can you send a Statement of Qualifications and references from comparable jobs?
  • How will you find the moisture source, and who fixes it?
  • How do you contain the work area and control air during removal?
  • Which materials will you remove, and which will you clean in place?
  • Do you follow ANSI/IICRC S520, and how does that show up in this job?
  • Who verifies the result, and how?
  • What is excluded, and who pulls permits for any structural, plumbing or wiring work?
  • Who will be on site each day, and what do they do?

A good company answers these plainly and in writing. Vague answers or pressure to sign today are signals to move on.

Write the answers down next to each company's name. A side-by-side page of notes makes the differences obvious a day later, when the sales conversations have blurred together.

What a good written scope contains

A written scope turns a sales conversation into something you can hold a contractor to. It should state where work happens, how the area will be sealed, what will be removed and cleaned and what will be left in place.

  • Locations and approximate extent of affected materials, with photos and moisture readings
  • Containment method and air control, including HEPA air scrubbers or negative air machines
  • Materials to be removed and materials to be cleaned
  • Cleaning method and drying equipment with the readings that define finished
  • Disposal method and who handles it
  • Verification method, and whether a separate tester is involved
  • Rebuild items included or excluded
  • Exclusions, including the moisture source and any trade work
  • Payment terms and how scope changes are documented

Standards worth asking about

ANSI/IICRC S520 is the published standard for professional mold remediation, and ANSI/IICRC S500 covers water damage restoration. They are industry documents that any homeowner can ask a company about. Do not treat them as a seal of approval. Treat them as a way to test whether the company can explain its method.

If a company cannot say how its containment, removal and verification steps relate to S520, ask for a plain-language description. The answer tells you how organized the job will be.

Using the state guide as your checklist in Holliston

What Massachusetts and Holliston give you to work with

The state Department of Public Health's homeowner guide advises asking contractors for a Statement of Qualifications and references because mold remediation is unlicensed in Massachusetts. It also describes detergent and water as the usual answer for light surface growth, and that no official limit defines a safe level. Use those points to decide whether you need a company at all.

If the job turns up plumbing, electrical or structural work, the Holliston Building Department handles those permits and inspections. Building modifications can also need Health Agent review. Ask any contractor who is responsible for permits before you sign.

Sources: Massachusetts Department of Public Health homeowner guide, Holliston Building Department website. Ask the Holliston Building Department about permit rules for your address.

When you may not need a company at all

A small patch of light growth on a tile, sealed trim or painted surface can often be cleaned with detergent and water, once the cause is fixed. If it is larger than that, or on drywall, insulation or a ceiling, treat it as a job for a crew. The state guide covers when to stop.

After a flood or sewage backup, do not clean porous materials yourself. Treat floodwater as contaminated, the state says, and plan to remove the porous finishes it touched. A flood damage restoration crew handles those jobs with proper protection.

What a good first visit looks like

A proper visit takes longer than a quick look. Expect someone to walk the whole affected area, take moisture readings on walls, floors and framing, photograph what they find and ask how and when the water got in.

They should open or probe suspect spots, such as the back of a baseboard or the underside of a ceiling, and explain what they cannot see yet. If hidden growth is likely, a good company says so and builds that into the scope as a possible change, with the trigger spelled out.

You should leave with a written scope, not a verbal promise. If the visit ends with an immediate price and a request for a deposit, slow down and compare.

Insurance and neighbors: a few last checks

Before hiring, read the mold terms in your policy and ask your insurer what it expects. Sudden and accidental water damage is often covered, while gradual leaks often are not. No contractor can promise how a claim will turn out.

Homeowners in Hopkinton and Medway face the same unlicensed market, so the same checklist applies. If a company will not put its scope, exclusions and qualifications in writing, keep looking.

Frequently asked questions

Do mold removal businesses need a license in Massachusetts?

No. The state Department of Public Health's homeowner guide says there are no licenses required to conduct mold remediation in Massachusetts. That is why it advises asking for a Statement of Qualifications and references.

What is a Statement of Qualifications?

A written summary of the company's training, experience and methods. It should describe the people who will do the work and the standards they follow, and it should come with references you can call.

Should the same company clean and test?

It is cleaner when verification is done by someone independent of the company doing the cleanup, because there is no conflict of interest. Ask how verification works before you sign.

Is a cheaper quote a bad sign?

Not by itself, but check what it leaves out. Missing containment, missing air filtration or a rebuild that is excluded can explain a lower number. Compare scopes line by line.

How quickly should a company respond?

Same-day response when available is reasonable to ask about, but nobody can responsibly promise a fixed arrival time. Prompt, clear communication and a written scope matter more than speed claims.

Who should I ask about permits for mold-related repairs in Holliston?

The Holliston Building Department handles building, plumbing, gas, wiring, sheet metal and trench permits and inspections. Building modifications can also need Health Agent review. Ask the contractor to name who pulls each permit.
